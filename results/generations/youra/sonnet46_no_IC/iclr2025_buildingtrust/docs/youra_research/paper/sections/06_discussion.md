# 6. Discussion

## 6.1 Key Findings and Their Interpretation

**RLHF as a pervasive alignment signal.** Our most striking finding is that RLHF fine-tuning simultaneously shapes 5 of 6 trustworthiness dimensions through a shared optimization signal. This is not merely the observation that "RLHF models score higher on trustworthiness" — it is a structural claim: after removing the scale confound, the 5 RLHF-shaped dimensions form a tight correlation cluster (ρ up to 0.971) that cannot be explained by general model quality. The within-family LLaMA-2 natural experiment provides the cleanest evidence: across 7B, 13B, and 70B scale points, RLHF consistently produces joint positive deltas for both safety and ethics, with no counterexamples.

The implication for evaluation practice is immediate: a practitioner who measures 6 dimensions independently is unknowingly measuring the RLHF signal 5 times and the robustness dimension once. The correlation structure suggests that measuring {truthfulness, fairness, privacy} — the MST hub nodes — provides approximately equivalent ranking power for the RLHF-shaped cluster, while adversarial robustness must be measured independently.

**Privacy is RLHF-sensitive.** Our original hypothesis classified privacy as RLHF-insensitive alongside robustness, reasoning that privacy protection is an architectural or data-driven concern. The data strongly refutes this: ρ(safety, privacy) = 0.971 is the strongest pair in the entire 15-pair matrix, and privacy lands firmly in the RLHF-shaped cluster. Two complementary explanations are plausible: (1) RLHF reward models explicitly penalize privacy-violating outputs (PII disclosure, sensitive information leakage) alongside harmful outputs through the same annotator judgments; (2) TrustLLM's privacy dimension captures output-level refusal to produce PII, which mechanistically overlaps with the safety refusal behavior that RLHF trains. Both explanations may be simultaneously true. This finding has implications for RLHF policy design: safety and privacy may be jointly addressable through shared training rather than requiring separate alignment efforts.

**Robustness isolation as an actionable finding.** Adversarial robustness is the only trustworthiness dimension that is not part of the RLHF-shaped cluster. This is consistent with the mechanistic intuition that adversarial examples are rarely represented in RLHF preference annotation data — annotators evaluate model responses to natural prompts, not adversarially perturbed inputs. As a result, robustness improvement requires separate dedicated effort (adversarial training, data augmentation) rather than being a byproduct of standard RLHF alignment. The 3/3 LLaMA-2 pairs showing negative Δ_robustness after RLHF fine-tuning are directionally consistent with a safety-robustness tension, though the effect does not reach statistical significance at n=16.

## 6.2 Limitations

**Sample size (n=16).** The TrustLLM evaluation set comprises 16 models. With 2 covariates, partial correlation tests have df=12, limiting power to detect moderate correlations (|ρ| < 0.55 cannot be distinguished from zero at Bonferroni-corrected α). The safety-robustness null finding (ρ=−0.188, p=0.519) is almost certainly a power issue rather than true independence: the scale-only ablation (ρ=−0.771, p=0.0008) confirms the anti-correlation exists, and all within-family comparisons show directionally negative robustness deltas. Replication with larger model sets (n≥30) is needed to resolve moderate effects.

**RLHF mechanism is proposed, not causally proven.** The RLHF explanation is the most parsimonious given the within-family evidence, but we cannot rule out alternative confounders in observational cross-model data. Better-trained models may receive more careful treatment across all safety-adjacent dimensions for reasons unrelated to RLHF reward signal design. We describe RLHF as the "most likely explanatory framework," not a proven causal mechanism.

**TrustLLM-specific operationalization.** Our findings are specific to the TrustLLM 2024 evaluation framework and its particular operationalization of each dimension. HELM uses different sub-benchmarks for safety (toxicity) and fairness (bias) that may not be construct-equivalent to TrustLLM's definitions. Cross-framework HELM replication — which we identify as important future work — would clarify whether the correlation structure is specific to TrustLLM's operationalization or generalizes across benchmark frameworks.

**Machine_ethics MST edge ambiguity.** The machine_ethics–privacy MST edge has 68.8% bootstrap frequency, reflecting genuine near-tie geometry at n=16: machine_ethics is approximately equidistant from privacy, safety, and fairness in the correlation space. The minimum evaluation *size* (3 dimensions) is stable; the specific edge topology involving machine_ethics is not. We report the mean per-edge bootstrap frequency (0.917) as the correct stability metric following Tumminello (2007), rather than the fraction of bootstrap MSTs with identical full topology (0.606, as computed in our initial H-E2 experiment).

## 6.3 Broader Impact

This work has implications for both research practice and deployment evaluation:

**For benchmark design.** Trustworthiness benchmarks that measure 6 independent dimensions may be reporting redundant information for 5 of those dimensions. Future benchmark design should account for the correlation structure, potentially reporting a "trustworthiness correlation score" alongside dimension scores to give practitioners a sense of which models are genuinely differentiated on the independent axis (robustness) vs the correlated cluster.

**For deployment evaluation.** Our MST-derived minimum evaluation set {truthfulness, fairness, privacy} provides a principled basis for evaluation compression in deployment screening. This is particularly relevant for organizations with limited evaluation budgets: measuring 3 dimensions instead of 6 can reduce evaluation cost by 50% while preserving ranking quality for the correlated cluster dimensions.

**For alignment research.** The privacy-safety coupling (ρ=0.971) suggests that constitutional AI and RLHF training may be jointly addressing privacy alongside safety without explicit privacy objectives being specified. This has implications for how we think about alignment generalization: a training signal targeting safety may have broader trustworthiness effects than currently appreciated.

**Potential negative uses.** Understanding which dimensions are correlated could theoretically help adversarial actors find models that score high on correlated dimensions (satisfying automated safety checks) while performing poorly on the independent dimension (robustness). We note that this risk exists regardless of whether the correlation structure is published — it is an empirical property of current models. Making the structure explicit and publishing the findings enables defensive countermeasures (targeted robustness evaluation) that are not possible without this knowledge.
