# Conclusion

We began by observing that GLUE, one of the most influential benchmarks in NLP history, was replaced by its successor within twelve months — and that no automated system detected the saturation that made this replacement necessary. This observation motivated a simple question: can the temporal dynamics of benchmark score progression, already embedded in public leaderboard data, be used to detect saturation automatically?

Our answer is yes, with meaningful precision. By formalizing benchmark score-over-time trajectories as logistic growth processes and applying AIC model selection to compare them against linear and power-law alternatives, we achieve decisive statistical justification for the logistic model (ΔAIC ≈ −194 to −357) and retrospective saturation detection within 3--5 months of community-recognized events for both GLUE and SuperGLUE. An empirically optimized detection criterion achieves one-month accuracy on both benchmarks.

**Summary of contributions.** We introduced the first automated, benchmark-agnostic pipeline for temporal saturation detection from public leaderboard timeseries. The pipeline requires no new test data collection, no domain-specific heuristics, and no human annotation — only the historical score-date records that benchmark leaderboards already make public. The core insight — that benchmark-specific overfitting accumulates in an S-curve pattern formally captured by the logistic growth model — transforms the qualitative concept of ``benchmark saturation'' into a quantitative, reproducible criterion. Along the way, we uncovered an unexpected finding: the logistic inflection point for both GLUE and SuperGLUE predates benchmark launch, suggesting that BERT-era pretraining had already saturated benchmark-exploitable signal before systematic leaderboard tracking began. We also demonstrated that the pipeline generalizes to benchmarks with as few as 30 leaderboard entries when bounded logistic fitting is used.

**Future directions.** Three directions emerge directly from our results.

First, the prospective forecasting failure (P3) has a clear root cause: a 6-month-early truncation of GLUE leaves only 14 months of data, insufficient for logistic plateau detection. Testing shorter lookback windows (1--3 months early truncation) and benchmarks with faster saturation dynamics would establish whether the method can be extended from retrospective monitoring to early warning.

Second, the negative $t_0$ finding requires independent verification. If live Papers With Code submission timestamps (accessible via the HuggingFace API, which has replaced the deprecated PwC client) show negative $t_0$ persisting for GLUE, the pre-launch saturation interpretation is strengthened. If live timestamps give positive $t_0$, the finding is more likely a curated data artifact. This experiment would take days, not months, and would substantially sharpen the mechanistic interpretation.

Third, the H-C1 boundary condition finding (pipeline works at ≥30 entries) is based entirely on synthetic benchmarks. Real validation on small benchmarks with diverse submission patterns and domain-specific variability is required before confidently recommending the scope relaxation from ≥50 to ≥30 entries.

**Broader vision.** The ML community currently treats benchmark retirement as an informal community event — a collective recognition that something has run its course. We envision a future in which benchmark health dashboards, powered by automated saturation detection across hundreds of leaderboards, provide continuous quantitative signals to the community: which benchmarks are in active growth phases, which are approaching saturation, and which have plateaued and should be succeeded. Our work establishes the mathematical and empirical foundation for such a system. Returning to where we started: if this pipeline had been running in 2019, it would have flagged GLUE's saturation within three months of the actual community-recognized event — and the negative $t_0$ finding suggests the signal was present even before the leaderboard opened. The benchmark was saturated before we started measuring it. That is, perhaps, the most important thing our pipeline can tell us.
