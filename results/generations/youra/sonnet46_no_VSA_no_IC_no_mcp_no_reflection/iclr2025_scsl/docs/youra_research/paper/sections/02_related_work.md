# Related Work

## Spurious Correlations and Worst-Group Accuracy

Neural networks trained with ERM exploit spurious correlations — dataset artifacts where simple co-occurring features predict class labels during training but fail to generalize across subpopulations. On Waterbirds [Wah et al., 2011] and CelebA [Liu et al., 2015], spurious correlations between background/hair color and class labels cause ERM models to achieve high average accuracy (97% and 95% respectively) while failing catastrophically on minority groups (worst-group accuracy 72% and 47%) [Sagawa et al., 2020]. The gap between average and worst-group accuracy is the canonical measure of spurious correlation reliance.

The theoretical basis for spurious correlation reliance is well-established. Shah et al. [2020] formally prove that gradient descent exhibits a simplicity bias: among features with equivalent training signal, the one with simpler representation (lower frequency, smaller parameter norm) is learned first and preferentially. Arpit et al. [2017] show that DNNs memorize easy-to-fit patterns before hard ones during training, producing temporal structure in the loss signal. These dynamics establish why spurious features — typically textures, backgrounds, or simpler statistical associations — are adopted as primary predictors before the model has opportunity to learn core causal features.

## Label-Free Debiasing Methods

Methods requiring group annotations (GroupDRO [Sagawa et al., 2020], CVaR-DRO [Levy et al., 2020]) achieve strong worst-group performance but assume the very label structure one wishes to avoid collecting. The practically important regime is annotation-free debiasing.

**Loss-based proxies.** Just Train Twice (JTT) [Liu et al., 2021] identifies the minority as the set of samples misclassified by an initial ERM model, then upweights them in a second training run — achieving 86.7% worst-group accuracy on Waterbirds without group labels. Learning from Failure (LfF) [Nam et al., 2020] uses a biased model's relative per-sample loss to identify spurious-minority samples online. Both methods succeed because per-sample loss is an effective proxy for spurious-minority membership: minority samples incur high loss precisely because the spurious feature that majority samples exploit conflicts with the true label for minority samples. However, this magnitude-based proxy conflates hard-majority samples — large gradient norm due to genuine task difficulty — with spurious-minority samples, a limitation noted by both Liu et al. [2021] and Kirichenko et al. [2022].

**Representation-based methods.** Deep Feature Reweighting (DFR) [Kirichenko et al., 2022] achieves near-label-supervised performance by retraining a linear head on a held-out balanced set, but requires a labelled validation split to define the balanced set — a weaker but non-trivial annotation requirement. CNC [Zhang et al., 2022] uses contrastive pairs but similarly requires some structure in label availability.

Our work extends the annotation-free regime to gradient *direction* as a proxy signal, testing whether the directional information in gradients distinguishes spurious-minority from hard-majority samples in a way that loss magnitude cannot. We are not proposing a competing method to JTT or LfF; rather, we empirically characterize whether the theoretical advantage of directional signals holds in practice, and identify why it does not with within-batch reference direction.

## Gradient-Based Training Dynamics Analysis

Per-sample gradient analysis has a growing role in understanding training dynamics. Katharopoulos and Fleuret [2018] use gradient norms for importance sampling to accelerate training. Toneva et al. [2019] track "forgetting events" — samples that oscillate between correct and incorrect classification — finding these correlate with rare or hard samples. Swayamdipta et al. [2020] characterize data cartography using per-epoch loss and correctness signals to identify easy/ambiguous/hard training instances.

Most relevant is the concurrent work "Bias Leaves a Gradient Trail" [arXiv:2605.28780, 2025], which demonstrates that gradient probes computed at specific layers can identify biased samples without group labels. Their approach uses gradient *direction* relative to a reference, closely related to our hypothesis. The key difference is reference direction: they use a class-level reference computed globally, whereas we test the per-batch mean as reference. Our results suggest the per-batch reference is the failure point — not the directional concept itself — which is consistent with their success using more stable global references.

Gradient alignment has also been studied in the context of catastrophic forgetting (Gradient Episodic Memory [Lopez-Paz and Ranzato, 2017]) and multi-task learning [Yu et al., 2020], where samples or tasks whose gradients conflict with a mean direction are detected as interfering. These settings differ from spurious correlation detection in that the reference direction is typically computed globally or across tasks, not per mini-batch — a design choice our failure analysis retroactively validates.

## Simplicity Bias and Early Training Dynamics

The simplicity bias [Shah et al., 2020] and the spectral bias of gradient descent [Rahaman et al., 2019] together predict that at early training epochs, gradient structure reflects low-complexity feature learning. For spurious correlation benchmarks, this means epoch-1 gradients should contain the strongest directional signal about spurious-feature alignment. Our results are consistent with this in one sense — the alignment signal is most extreme at epoch 1 — but the inversion (ROC-AUC = 0.15) indicates the within-batch reference direction amplifies the contamination effect at precisely the epoch when gradient magnitudes are largest, making early-epoch gradient alignment the least reliable time to apply within-batch cosine similarity as a minority proxy.

**Our work directly fills the gap:** no prior study has measured the ROC-AUC of within-batch gradient cosine similarity as a spurious-minority detector on Waterbirds or CelebA across training epochs. We provide that measurement, characterize its failure mode, and identify the reference direction correction required for viable gradient-based minority detection.
