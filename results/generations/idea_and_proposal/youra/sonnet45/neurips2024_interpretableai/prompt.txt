## Interpretable AI: Past, Present and Future

Interpretability in machine learning revolves around constructing models that are inherently transparent and insightful for human end users. As the scale of machine learning models increases and the range of applications expands across diverse fields, the need for interpretable models is more crucial than ever. The significance of interpretability becomes particularly evident in scenarios where decisions carry substantial real-world consequences, influencing human lives in areas such as healthcare, criminal justice, and lending, where understanding the machine learning process is essential. Interpretability can aid in auditing, verification, debugging, bias detection, ensure safety, and align models more effectively with human intentions. Post-hoc explanations may be unfaithful and thereby unreliable in some applications, which is why it is essential to design inherently interpretable models that provide truthful and complete explanations by default. Motivated by this, researchers have studied interpretability, resulting in a spectrum of distinct approaches.

On one end of the spectrum, classical interpretability methods designed for small-scale and tabular datasets often use rule-based models (e.g., decision trees, risk scores) and linear models (e.g., sparse linear models, generalized linear models) that are deemed inherently transparent. On the other end, modern interpretability methods for large-scale foundation models involve incorporating interpretable components into deep neural networks while not being fully interpretable, spawning novel research areas such as mechanistic interpretability.

# Title
Unified Interpretability Evaluation Framework: Standardizing AI Transparency Assessment Across Model Scales and Paradigms

# Motivation
As AI systems scale from tabular models to foundation models and deploy across high-stakes domains (healthcare, criminal justice), interpretability evaluation remains fragmented and inconsistent. Different model scales (tabular vs. neural vs. transformers) and paradigms (rule-based vs. attribution-based vs. mechanistic interpretability) lack standardized assessment methods, making it impossible to reliably compare interpretability approaches or ensure regulatory compliance. This evaluation gap undermines trust in AI systems where transparency is critical for safety and accountability.

# Main Idea
We propose the Unified Interpretability Evaluation Framework (UIEF), applying multidimensional item response theory (MIRT) from psychometrics to enable "model-free interpretability measurement." The framework operates through a five-stage mechanism: (1) computing paradigm-specific atomic metrics, (2) adaptively calibrating model complexity and metric difficulty via MIRT, (3) translating metrics across scales using semantic-preserving functions that maintain properties like locality and faithfulness, (4) hierarchically aggregating metrics from atomic to holistic scores, and (5) applying domain-specific weighting for healthcare, autonomous systems, etc. We will validate through five studies testing cross-scale consistency (ρ>0.6), MIRT calibration accuracy (ρ>0.7), and practical utility (>15% improvement in method selection). This enables automated, reproducible interpretability assessment and regulatory compliance checking.

## 1. Hypothesis Statement

### Main Hypothesis

A Unified Interpretability Evaluation Framework (UIEF) based on multidimensional item response theory (MIRT), hierarchical metric composition, and semantic-preserving scale translation functions can provide consistent, standardized interpretability assessment across diverse model scales, paradigms, and application domains, thereby addressing the critical evaluation standardization gap in interpretability research.

**Mathematical Formulation:**
```
I_overall = F_aggregate(
    M_L3(M_L2(M_L1(model, data, paradigm) × W_domain)),
    θ_model, Δ_metric
)
```

**Key Components:**
- **M_L1:** Atomic metrics (paradigm-specific: rule fidelity, faithfulness score, circuit completeness)
- **M_L2:** Composite metrics (cross-paradigm: explanation consistency, human alignment)
- **M_L3:** Holistic metrics (overall trustworthiness index)
- **θ_model:** Model complexity parameter (MIRT-estimated)
- **Δ_metric:** Metric difficulty parameter (MIRT-calibrated)
- **W_domain:** Domain-specific weighting vector (healthcare, criminal justice, etc.)

### Alternative Hypothesis (H0)

Interpretability evaluation cannot be standardized across scales and paradigms; each model scale and paradigm requires fundamentally incompatible evaluation approaches, making unified assessment theoretically impossible or practically uninformative.

## 2. Core Mechanism

**Five-Stage Causal Chain:**

1. **Atomic Measurement (L1):** Paradigm-specific metrics computed (e.g., LIME faithfulness score for attribution-based methods)

2. **Adaptive Calibration (MIRT):** Model complexity θ_model and metric difficulty Δ_metric estimated via multidimensional IRT
   - P(metric_applicable | model) = f(θ_model - Δ_metric)
   - Filters out inappropriate metrics (e.g., avoids applying circuit analysis to tabular models)

3. **Scale Translation (if cross-scale):** Semantic-preserving functions map metrics across scales
   - Example: f_locality(feature_importance [tabular] → saliency [neural]) preserves "which input region matters"
   - Ontology: {locality, faithfulness, stability, plausibility} preserved

4. **Hierarchical Aggregation (L1 → L2 → L3):** Atomic metrics compose into composite, then holistic scores
   - Explicit composition rules (not black-box)
   - Enables both detailed (L1) and summary (L3) assessments

5. **Domain Adaptation:** Domain-specific weighting W_domain adjusts metric importance
   - Example: Healthcare emphasizes transparency (0.4), fairness (0.3); Autonomous systems emphasizes real-time (0.5)
