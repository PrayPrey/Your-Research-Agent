# Sampling and Optimization in Discrete Space

## Overview

Sampling and optimization in discrete space are classical and important problems that arise in many applications, including physics, combinatorial optimization, compiler optimization, and many modern machine learning models like large language models and protein models. Examples include searching device placement strategies for distributed neural network training, or sampling from language model posteriors with arbitrary conditioning.

## Challenges

However, discrete space sampling/optimization is hard in general compared to continuous space. Although some independence structures can be leveraged for some special problems, in general discrete space sampling/optimization is slow. Recently, there have been new research trends in efficient discrete sampling and optimization via

- Leveraging the gradient information of objectives: the gradient based MCMC algorithms generalize the Langevin dynamics to discrete space.
- Embedding into a continuous space: The embedding methods first map the discrete space to continuous space, then apply an efficient sampling algorithm in continuous space, then map the new sample back into discrete space.
- Other effective proposal strategies in discrete space: for example the Stein variational method and GFlowNet. These methods have improved efficiency in many domains. With simulated annealing, they can also serve as optimization algorithms and demonstrate superior performance on many combinatorial optimization problems compared to existing learning-based methods. However, they might still be limited when applied to black-box objectives, or problems involving long-range and high-order correlations like in modern language models.

## Scope and Topics

- Syncing up on the latest research progress in discrete sampling and optimization.

We hope this workshop will be a great opportunity for presenting and discussing new algorithms and applications with researchers and practitioners within or outside the domain of discrete sampling/optimization.

# Title
Sparse Bayesian Epistasis Networks for Sample-Efficient Black-Box Discrete Optimization

# Motivation
Discrete optimization problems with expensive black-box objectives (>$100/evaluation) and epistatic interactions—where variables interact non-additively—are critical in protein engineering, molecular design, and compiler optimization. Current methods face a fundamental trade-off: VAE-based approaches detect epistasis but require 2,000-5,000 evaluations, while GFlowNets achieve sample efficiency (hundreds of samples) but model interactions implicitly. This makes wet-lab applications ($1,000/sample) prohibitively expensive. A method that explicitly detects epistatic interactions with <500 evaluations would enable previously infeasible experimental campaigns.

# Main Idea
We propose Sparse Bayesian Epistasis Networks (SBEN), combining spike-and-slab priors for sparse structure learning, hierarchical expansion (pairwise→triplet interactions), and active learning. The causal mechanism operates through four steps: (1) sparse priors concentrate search on likely interactions, reducing complexity from O(n²) to O(cn); (2) hierarchical expansion adaptively discovers high-order interactions only when pairwise models fail (R²<0.7); (3) active learning selects uncertainty-reducing samples via variance-based acquisition; (4) combined mechanisms achieve epistasis detection with <500 evaluations—a 4-10× improvement over VAE-memetic baselines. We test on NK landscapes (k=3) and protein fitness benchmarks, with falsification if sample requirements exceed 500 evaluations or show no advantage over existing methods.

### 1.3 Causal Mechanism

**Step 1: Sparse Prior Selection**
Spike-and-slab priors enforce sparsity in the Bayesian network structure, concentrating probability mass on small subsets of possible interactions. This reduces the effective search space from O(n²) to O(cn) where c is expected edge count, enabling efficient structure learning with limited samples.

**Step 2: Hierarchical Expansion (L2 → L3)**
Starting with pairwise interactions (L2), the method computes a sufficiency test (R² > 0.7). If pairwise interactions explain observed variance, higher-order search is skipped. Otherwise, expansion to L3 (triplets) occurs only in variable clusters with high residual error. This adaptive expansion maintains O(n²) complexity in typical sparse cases while handling genuine high-order interactions when present.

**Step 3: Active Learning Guidance**
Active learning selects next samples using variance reduction criterion (cheap approximation to mutual information about edge weights). This concentrates evaluations on uncertainty-reducing samples rather than uniform exploration, accelerating interaction discovery by 3x compared to random sampling.

**Step 4: Outcome - Sample-Efficient Epistasis Detection**
The combination of sparse priors + hierarchical expansion + active learning achieves epistasis detection with <500 samples, enabling optimization on expensive black-box objectives (e.g., wet-lab protein synthesis at $1000/sample) that are currently infeasible with VAE-based methods requiring thousands of samples.

### 1.4 Key Assumptions

1. **Sparsity Assumption**: The true interaction graph is sparse, with most discrete variables not interacting directly.
   - **Evidence**: Biological systems and molecular structures typically exhibit local interactions (Alon 2007 on network motifs).
   - **Consequence if violated**: If interaction graph is fully connected, O(n²) hierarchical approach degrades to O(n^k) exhaustive search, losing sample efficiency advantage.

2. **Hierarchical Epistasis**: High-order interactions (L3+) are rarer than pairwise interactions (L2).
   - **Evidence**: Quantum chemistry perturbation theory demonstrates first-order effects dominate over second-order; parallel observed in genetic epistasis (Phillips 2008).
   - **Consequence if violated**: If L3/L4 interactions dominate without L2 foundation, hierarchical expansion will waste samples on uninformative pairwise search before discovering relevant high-order structure.

3. **Reliable Black-Box Evaluation**: The objective function can be evaluated reliably at least 500 times within budget/time constraints.
   - **Evidence**: Protein fitness assays can be automated; NK landscapes are deterministic; computational objectives allow unlimited evaluations.
   - **Consequence if violated**: If <500 evaluations are feasible (e.g., $500K budget, $1000/sample), method cannot converge; alternative methods required (transfer learning, simulation).

4. **Bayesian Inference Tractability**: Spike-and-slab inference and structure learning scale to problem size n=100-1000.
   - **Evidence**: PyMC3 and pgmpy libraries handle sparse Bayesian networks up to n~1000 variables; variational inference approximations enable scaling.
   - **Consequence if violated**: If inference becomes intractable (>24 hours/iteration), active learning loop cannot iterate sufficiently within sample budget, reducing sample efficiency gains.
