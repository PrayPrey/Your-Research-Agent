# Research Proposal: Crowdsourced Stakeholder-Centric Evaluation Frameworks for Generative AI Impacts

## 1. Introduction

### Background

Generative AI systems—capable of producing text, images, audio, and video content—are rapidly permeating every sector of society, from education and healthcare to creative industries and journalism. As these systems become ubiquitous, their broader societal impacts extend far beyond technical performance metrics. While initiatives like the NeurIPS Broader Impact statement have catalyzed awareness of potential negative consequences, the AI research community lacks standardized, comprehensive methodologies for assessing these impacts. Current evaluation practices remain predominantly confined within machine learning research communities, prioritizing technical benchmarks such as perplexity, FID scores, and accuracy metrics while inadequately capturing real-world harms experienced by affected populations.

This disconnect between technical evaluation and lived experience represents a critical gap in responsible AI development. Content creators face economic displacement from AI-generated media; educators grapple with academic integrity challenges posed by large language models; healthcare workers encounter misinformation generated by AI systems; and marginalized communities disproportionately bear the burden of biased outputs and representational harms. These stakeholders possess invaluable contextual knowledge about AI impacts that researchers operating in isolation cannot fully anticipate or understand.

Recent scholarship has begun addressing this gap. Chouldechova et al. (2024) introduced a shared standard for measuring generative AI capabilities and risks, emphasizing stakeholder perspective integration. Weidinger et al. (2023) proposed a three-layered sociotechnical safety evaluation framework incorporating human interaction and systemic impacts. Long (2025) advocated for Bayesian statistical approaches to facilitate stakeholder participation. However, no comprehensive, actionable protocol currently exists for systematically incorporating diverse stakeholder voices into impact assessments at scale.

### Research Objectives

This research proposes developing a **Participatory Evaluation Protocol (PEP)** that bridges the expertise gap between AI researchers and affected communities. Our specific objectives are:

1. To create a comprehensive **Stakeholder Mapping Taxonomy** that systematically categorizes affected groups across different generative AI modalities and application contexts.

2. To design and validate **Modular Evaluation Templates** consisting of standardized yet adaptable instruments enabling non-ML experts to report observed impacts with methodological rigor.

3. To develop an **Aggregation Pipeline** that synthesizes qualitative stakeholder feedback into quantifiable impact indicators complementing existing technical benchmarks.

4. To empirically validate the framework through comparative studies examining stakeholder-identified harms versus researcher-anticipated harms across multiple generative AI applications.

### Significance

This research directly addresses the NeurIPS workshop's key focus on broadening participation in AI evaluation. By creating reproducible, community-accessible resources, we enable impact assessments that reflect diverse lived experiences rather than solely technical considerations. The resulting framework will serve as a foundational community resource, fostering collaboration between AI researchers, practitioners, policymakers, and affected communities while establishing precedents for inclusive evaluation standards.

## 2. Methodology

### 2.1 Stakeholder Mapping Taxonomy Development

#### Phase 1: Literature Synthesis and Expert Consultation

We will conduct a systematic review of existing stakeholder categorization frameworks in technology assessment, participatory design, and AI ethics literature. Drawing from the frameworks proposed by Weidinger et al. (2023) and methodologies for stakeholder engagement discussed in recent literature (2023), we will synthesize existing categorization approaches.

Subsequently, we will convene a Delphi study with 15-20 experts spanning AI ethics, sociology, policy studies, and domain-specific fields (education, healthcare, creative industries). Through iterative rounds, experts will refine stakeholder categories until consensus emerges.

#### Phase 2: Taxonomy Structure

The taxonomy will employ a three-dimensional framework:

**Dimension 1: Proximity to AI System**
- *Direct Users*: Individuals actively interacting with generative AI tools
- *Indirect Affected Parties*: Those impacted by AI outputs without direct interaction
- *Systemic Stakeholders*: Institutions and communities affected at structural levels

**Dimension 2: Vulnerability Status**
- *General Population*: Stakeholders without elevated risk factors
- *Vulnerable Populations*: Groups facing heightened risks due to socioeconomic status, disability, age, or historical marginalization

**Dimension 3: Modality-Specific Categories**

For each generative AI modality $m \in \{text, image, audio, video\}$, we define stakeholder sets $S_m$ with associated impact domains $D_m$. The complete taxonomy is represented as:

$$T = \{(s, v, m, d) : s \in S, v \in V, m \in M, d \in D_m\}$$

where $S$ represents stakeholder types, $V$ represents vulnerability status, $M$ represents modalities, and $D_m$ represents domain-specific impact categories.

### 2.2 Modular Evaluation Template Design

#### Instrument Development

We will develop three complementary instrument types:

**Type A: Structured Survey Instruments**

Quantitative surveys using validated scales measuring perceived impact across dimensions including:
- Economic impact (employment, income effects)
- Psychological impact (trust, anxiety, agency)
- Social impact (relationships, community cohesion)
- Informational impact (access to accurate information, epistemic effects)

Each item $i$ will be measured on a 7-point Likert scale, with composite scores calculated as:

$$\text{Impact Score}_d = \frac{1}{n_d} \sum_{i \in d} w_i \cdot r_i$$

where $d$ represents the impact domain, $n_d$ is the number of items in domain $d$, $w_i$ represents item weights determined through factor analysis, and $r_i$ represents individual responses.

**Type B: Semi-Structured Interview Protocols**

Qualitative protocols enabling in-depth exploration of experienced harms, unexpected benefits, and contextual factors. Protocols will follow a funnel structure: opening with broad questions about AI encounters, narrowing to specific impact experiences, and concluding with recommendations for improvement.

**Type C: Incident Reporting Forms**

Streamlined instruments for documenting specific harm incidents, adapted from existing red-teaming methodologies (2024). Forms capture: incident description, affected parties, severity assessment, temporal characteristics, and potential mitigation strategies.

#### Validation Process

Instruments will undergo rigorous validation through:

1. **Content Validity**: Expert panel review (n=12) assessing item relevance and comprehensiveness
2. **Construct Validity**: Exploratory and confirmatory factor analysis using pilot data (n=300)
3. **Reliability Assessment**: Internal consistency (Cronbach's α > 0.7) and test-retest reliability (ICC > 0.75)
4. **Accessibility Testing**: Cognitive interviews with non-ML experts (n=30) to ensure comprehensibility

### 2.3 Aggregation Pipeline Development

#### Qualitative-to-Quantitative Transformation

We propose a multi-stage aggregation pipeline transforming diverse stakeholder inputs into standardized impact indicators:

**Stage 1: Thematic Coding**

Qualitative responses undergo systematic coding using a hybrid deductive-inductive approach. Initial codes derive from the taxonomy framework, supplemented by emergent codes from stakeholder narratives. Inter-rater reliability will be established (Cohen's κ > 0.8).

**Stage 2: Sentiment and Severity Analysis**

Natural language processing techniques extract sentiment polarity and severity indicators from textual responses. We employ a fine-tuned transformer model calibrated against human annotations:

$$\text{Severity}_i = f_\theta(\text{response}_i)$$

where $f_\theta$ represents the calibrated model outputting severity scores on a continuous [0,1] scale.

**Stage 3: Weighted Aggregation**

Individual stakeholder inputs are aggregated into composite impact indicators using a weighted scheme accounting for stakeholder proximity, vulnerability status, and sample representation:

$$\text{Impact Indicator}_d = \sum_{s \in S} \pi_s \cdot \left( \frac{\sum_{i \in s} \text{Severity}_{i,d}}{|s|} \right)$$

where $\pi_s$ represents sampling weights ensuring representational balance across stakeholder categories.

**Stage 4: Bayesian Integration**

Following Long (2025), we employ Bayesian methods to integrate stakeholder feedback with technical benchmarks:

$$P(\text{Impact} | \text{Stakeholder}, \text{Technical}) \propto P(\text{Stakeholder} | \text{Impact}) \cdot P(\text{Technical} | \text{Impact}) \cdot P(\text{Impact})$$

This formulation enables principled uncertainty quantification and allows updating impact assessments as new evidence accumulates.

### 2.4 Empirical Validation Study

#### Application Domains

We will validate the framework across four generative AI applications:
1. **Text Generation**: Large language models in educational contexts
2. **Image Generation**: AI art tools affecting creative professionals
3. **Audio Generation**: Voice synthesis systems impacting content creators
4. **Video Generation**: Deepfake detection challenges in journalism

#### Participant Recruitment

For each domain, we will recruit:
- 100 direct users through platform partnerships
- 75 indirect affected parties through community organizations
- 50 representatives from vulnerable populations through advocacy groups
- 25 domain experts for comparison benchmarking

Total sample: N = 1,000 participants across all domains.

#### Comparative Analysis

The core empirical contribution involves comparing stakeholder-identified harms with researcher-anticipated harms. Prior to stakeholder data collection, we will conduct a systematic survey of AI researchers (n=50) to document anticipated harms for each application domain.

Comparison metrics include:
- **Coverage Ratio**: $CR = \frac{|\text{Stakeholder Harms} \cap \text{Researcher Harms}|}{|\text{Stakeholder Harms}|}$
- **Novel Harm Discovery Rate**: Proportion of stakeholder-identified harms not anticipated by researchers
- **Severity Calibration**: Correlation between researcher-estimated and stakeholder-reported severity

#### Evaluation Metrics

Framework effectiveness will be assessed through:
1. **Comprehensiveness**: Taxonomy coverage of empirically observed impacts
2. **Accessibility**: Time-to-completion and comprehension scores for non-experts
3. **Reliability**: Inter-instrument consistency across template types
4. **Validity**: Correlation with externally validated impact measures
5. **Actionability**: Expert assessment of recommendation specificity and feasibility

## 3. Expected Outcomes & Impact

### Deliverables

1. **Stakeholder Mapping Taxonomy**: A comprehensive, publicly available classification system for generative AI stakeholders, disseminated through interactive web documentation and academic publication.

2. **Modular Evaluation Toolkit**: Open-source repository containing validated survey instruments, interview protocols, and incident reporting forms in multiple languages, with accompanying implementation guides.

3. **Aggregation Pipeline Software**: Documented codebase implementing the qualitative-to-quantitative transformation process, including trained NLP models and Bayesian integration modules.

4. **Empirical Findings Report**: Comprehensive analysis comparing stakeholder-identified versus researcher-anticipated harms, identifying systematic blind spots in current evaluation practices.

5. **Policy Recommendations**: Evidence-based guidelines for incorporating participatory evaluation into AI development lifecycles and regulatory frameworks.

### Broader Impact

This research addresses fundamental inequities in AI governance by democratizing evaluation processes. By creating accessible tools enabling non-experts to contribute meaningfully to impact assessments, we shift power dynamics from exclusively technical communities toward affected populations.

The framework establishes precedents for participatory approaches that could extend beyond generative AI to emerging technologies more broadly. Furthermore, empirical findings documenting discrepancies between researcher-anticipated and stakeholder-identified harms will inform more responsible research practices and funding priorities.

For the NeurIPS community specifically, this work provides actionable resources for researchers seeking to conduct more inclusive Broader Impact assessments. The toolkit enables systematic stakeholder consultation that complements technical evaluation, creating a more holistic picture of AI system effects.

Ultimately, this research contributes to building AI systems that serve diverse human needs—not merely those anticipated by their creators—fostering technology development that is accountable, inclusive, and responsive to the communities it affects.
