# Research Proposal: Adaptive Curriculum Learning for Self-Improving Foundation Models via Difficulty-Aware Synthetic Data Generation

## 1. Title

**Adaptive Curriculum Learning for Self-Improving Foundation Models via Difficulty-Aware Synthetic Data Generation**

## 2. Introduction

### 2.1 Background

The rapid advancement of foundation models (FMs), particularly large language models (LLMs), has been primarily driven by scaling model parameters and training data. However, the machine learning community is approaching a critical juncture: high-quality internet data is finite and growing slower than the consumption needs of modern FMs during pre-training. Recent evidence suggests that gains from simply scaling pre-training data are diminishing, while test-time techniques and self-improvement mechanisms show increasing promise. This data bottleneck is even more acute in domains like robotics and embodied AI, where real-world interaction data remains severely limited.

Self-improvement—the paradigm where models train on synthetic data generated by themselves or other models—offers a potential solution to this data scarcity problem. However, this approach introduces unique challenges distinct from traditional supervised learning and reinforcement learning. Unlike supervised learning with curated human annotations, self-improvement requires the model to generate its own training signal. Unlike traditional RL with external environment feedback, self-improvement must contend with learned verifiers that can fail arbitrarily, and the risk of model collapse where models reinforce their own biases and errors.

Recent work has highlighted critical failure modes in self-improvement: models can suffer from distribution drift, where repeated training on self-generated data leads to convergence toward low-entropy, overconfident outputs that fail to capture the true data distribution. This "model collapse" phenomenon occurs when models are trained indiscriminately on their own outputs without principled selection mechanisms.

### 2.2 Research Gap

While existing approaches to self-improvement have made progress—such as SPIN's self-play mechanism and R-Few's challenger-solver framework—they suffer from fundamental limitations:

1. **Static Difficulty Assessment**: Current methods either generate synthetic data uniformly across the problem space or rely on fixed difficulty metrics that don't adapt to the model's evolving capabilities.

2. **Lack of Principled Curriculum**: Existing approaches don't systematically target the "edge of competence"—the zone where learning is most efficient—instead treating all synthetic data equally.

3. **Insufficient Quality Control**: Verification mechanisms often lack robustness, failing to account for verifier uncertainty and ensemble disagreement.

4. **No Theoretical Guarantees**: Most self-improvement methods lack formal analysis of convergence properties or bounds on distribution drift.

### 2.3 Research Objectives

This research proposes an **Adaptive Curriculum Learning (ACL)** framework that addresses these gaps through four key innovations:

1. Develop a **capability mapping mechanism** that continuously identifies the model's uncertainty boundaries across the problem space.

2. Design an **adaptive synthetic data generation** strategy that dynamically targets problems at the edge of the model's competence.

3. Implement **ensemble-based quality gating** with principled confidence thresholds to prevent model collapse.

4. Establish **theoretical guarantees** on convergence and distribution preservation under defined conditions.

### 2.4 Significance

This research has significant implications for scaling foundation models beyond current data limitations:

- **Sample Efficiency**: By focusing on high-value synthetic data, we reduce computational waste and accelerate self-improvement.
- **Robustness**: Quality gating mechanisms prevent model collapse and ensure stable long-term learning.
- **Generalizability**: The framework applies across domains where verification is tractable (mathematical reasoning, code generation, theorem proving, etc.).
- **Theoretical Foundation**: Formal analysis provides confidence in deployment and identifies boundary conditions for safe self-improvement.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Problem Formulation

Let $\mathcal{M}_t$ denote a foundation model at iteration $t$, with parameters $\theta_t$. The model operates over a problem space $\mathcal{X}$ with solutions in space $\mathcal{Y}$. We assume access to:

1. A small seed dataset $\mathcal{D}_0 = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{N_0}$ of high-quality human-annotated examples
2. A verification function $V: \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y} \rightarrow \{0, 1\}$ (or verifier model ensemble)
3. The model's own generation capability $p_{\theta_t}(y|x)$

Our goal is to design a curriculum $\mathcal{C} = \{\mathcal{D}_1, \mathcal{D}_2, ..., \mathcal{D}_T\}$ of synthetic datasets that maximizes long-term model performance while preventing distribution collapse.

### 3.2 Capability Mapping Module

#### 3.2.1 Uncertainty Quantification

We maintain an **uncertainty estimator** that identifies regions of the problem space where the model exhibits high epistemic uncertainty. For each problem $x \in \mathcal{X}$, we compute:

$$U(x; \mathcal{M}_t) = \mathbb{H}[p_{\theta_t}(y|x)] + \mathbb{V}_{y \sim p_{\theta_t}}[s(x, y)]$$

where $\mathbb{H}[\cdot]$ denotes entropy, and $s(x, y)$ is a confidence score from an ensemble of verifiers. The first term captures output diversity, while the second captures verifier disagreement.

#### 3.2.2 Capability Boundary Estimation

We partition the problem space into discrete difficulty levels $\{L_1, L_2, ..., L_K\}$ and estimate the model's capability frontier:

$$F_t = \{L_k : \mathbb{E}_{x \sim L_k}[U(x; \mathcal{M}_t)] \geq \tau_u \text{ and } \mathbb{E}_{x \sim L_k}[\text{Acc}(x; \mathcal{M}_t)] \geq \tau_a\}$$

where $\tau_u$ and $\tau_a$ are tunable thresholds. This identifies difficulty levels where the model shows moderate uncertainty but non-trivial accuracy—the optimal learning zone.

#### 3.2.3 Problem Embedding and Clustering

To enable fine-grained difficulty assessment, we embed problems using the model's internal representations:

$$e_x = \text{MeanPool}(\mathcal{M}_t^{\text{encoder}}(x))$$

We apply clustering (e.g., k-means) to identify problem clusters $\{C_1, ..., C_M\}$ and compute cluster-level uncertainty statistics:

$$U_j = \frac{1}{|C_j|}\sum_{x \in C_j} U(x; \mathcal{M}_t)$$

This enables targeted generation in high-uncertainty clusters.

### 3.3 Adaptive Synthetic Data Generation

#### 3.3.1 Difficulty-Aware Sampling

At iteration $t$, we generate synthetic problems by:

1. **Cluster Selection**: Sample clusters with probability proportional to uncertainty:
$$p(C_j) \propto \exp(\beta \cdot U_j)$$
where $\beta$ is a temperature parameter controlling exploration-exploitation.

2. **Problem Generation**: Use the model as a challenger to generate problems in selected clusters. We employ a **Chain-of-Thought problem design** mechanism:

$$x_{\text{new}} \sim p_{\theta_t}(\cdot | \text{prompt}(C_j, \mathcal{D}_{\text{exemplar}}))$$

where the prompt includes cluster characteristics and exemplar problems from $C_j$.

3. **Difficulty Control**: Adjust generation temperature $\tau_{\text{gen}}$ based on target difficulty:

$$\tau_{\text{gen}} = \tau_0 \cdot (1 + \alpha \cdot \frac{U_j - \bar{U}}{\sigma_U})$$

Higher temperatures increase diversity for uncertain regions.

#### 3.3.2 Solution Generation with Constrained Decoding

For each generated problem $x_{\text{new}}$, sample multiple candidate solutions:

$$\{y^{(1)}, ..., y^{(K)}\} \sim p_{\theta_t}(\cdot | x_{\text{new}})$$

We employ **constrained decoding** to encourage diverse reasoning paths while maintaining coherence, using nucleus sampling with dynamic $p$-values based on local uncertainty.

### 3.4 Ensemble-Based Quality Gating

#### 3.4.1 Verifier Ensemble

We maintain an ensemble of $N_v$ independent verifier models $\{V_1, ..., V_{N_v}\}$, each trained on different subsets of verified data. For a candidate pair $(x, y)$:

$$\text{Verification Score: } S(x, y) = \frac{1}{N_v}\sum_{i=1}^{N_v} V_i(x, y)$$

$$\text{Verifier Confidence: } C(x, y) = 1 - \frac{4}{N_v}\sum_{i=1}^{N_v}(V_i(x, y) - S(x,y))^2$$

High confidence requires both high average score and low verifier disagreement.

#### 3.4.2 Multi-Stage Filtering

Synthetic data passes through three gates:

1. **Correctness Gate**: $S(x, y) \geq \tau_s$ (typically $\tau_s = 0.8$)
2. **Confidence Gate**: $C(x, y) \geq \tau_c$ (typically $\tau_c = 0.7$)
3. **Novelty Gate**: $\min_{(x', y') \in \mathcal{D}_t} d((x, y), (x', y')) \geq \tau_d$

where $d(\cdot, \cdot)$ is embedding distance. Only data passing all three gates enters the training set.

#### 3.4.3 Adaptive Threshold Scheduling

Thresholds evolve over time to maintain data quality while ensuring sufficient volume:

$$\tau_s^{(t+1)} = \tau_s^{(t)} + \eta_s \cdot \mathbb{1}[|\mathcal{D}_{t+1}^{\text{synthetic}}| > N_{\text{target}}]$$

If too much data passes, increase selectivity; if too little, relax thresholds.

### 3.5 Curriculum Scheduling and Training

#### 3.5.1 Difficulty Progression

The curriculum advances through difficulty levels using a **mastery-based progression**:

$$\text{Advance to } L_{k+1} \text{ if } \mathbb{E}_{x \sim L_k}[\text{Acc}(x; \mathcal{M}_t)] \geq \tau_{\text{mastery}}$$

where $\tau_{\text{mastery}} = 0.85$. This ensures the model achieves competence before facing harder problems.

#### 3.5.2 Mixed Data Training

At each iteration, construct training set:

$$\mathcal{D}_t = \mathcal{D}_0 \cup \mathcal{D}_t^{\text{synthetic}}$$

with mixing ratio $\lambda_t$ controlling the proportion of synthetic data:

$$\lambda_t = \min(0.9, \lambda_0 + \gamma \cdot t)$$

We gradually increase synthetic data proportion but maintain human data to anchor the distribution.

#### 3.5.3 Training Objective

Optimize using a combined loss:

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y) \sim \mathcal{D}_t}[-\log p_\theta(y|x)] + \lambda_{\text{reg}} \cdot D_{\text{KL}}(p_\theta || p_{\theta_{\text{ref}}})$$

where $p_{\theta_{\text{ref}}}$ is a reference model (the initial pretrained model) and the KL term prevents excessive distribution drift—a key regularizer against collapse.

### 3.6 Experimental Design

#### 3.6.1 Domains and Datasets

We evaluate on three domains with tractable verification:

1. **Mathematical Reasoning**: GSM8K, MATH dataset (competition mathematics)
2. **Code Generation**: HumanEval, APPS (programming problems with test cases)
3. **Theorem Proving**: miniF2F (formal mathematics in Lean)

For each domain, split data into:
- Seed set: 10% of training data
- Validation set: 15% for hyperparameter tuning
- Test set: held-out for final evaluation

#### 3.6.2 Baselines

Compare against:

1. **Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT)**: Train only on seed data
2. **Uniform Self-Improvement**: Generate synthetic data uniformly without curriculum
3. **SPIN**: Self-play fine-tuning (Chen et al., 2024)
4. **R-Few**: Challenger-solver with static curriculum (Yu et al., 2025)
5. **Standard RL**: Proximal Policy Optimization with learned reward model

#### 3.6.3 Implementation Details

- **Base Models**: Llama-3-8B, DeepSeek-Math-7B
- **Training**: 5 iterations of curriculum, 2 epochs per iteration
- **Generation**: Sample 10K synthetic problems per iteration, keep top 5K after filtering
- **Verifiers**: Ensemble of 5 models, each trained on 80% bootstrap sample
- **Compute**: 8× A100 GPUs, ~200 GPU-hours per full run

#### 3.6.4 Evaluation Metrics

**Performance Metrics**:
- **Accuracy**: Pass@1 on held-out test sets
- **Sample Efficiency**: Accuracy vs. number of synthetic samples used
- **Generalization**: Transfer to related but distinct benchmarks

**Distribution Health Metrics**:
- **Output Diversity**: $\mathbb{H}[p_\theta(y|x)]$ averaged over test set
- **Distribution Drift**: $D_{\text{KL}}(p_\theta || p_{\text{data}})$ estimated via classifier-based method
- **Verifier Agreement**: Consistency of ensemble predictions over time

**Curriculum Quality Metrics**:
- **Difficulty Alignment**: Correlation between generated problem difficulty and model capability
- **Coverage**: Proportion of problem space clusters represented in synthetic data

### 3.7 Theoretical Analysis

#### 3.7.1 Convergence Guarantees

We establish that under assumptions of:
1. Bounded verifier error rate $\epsilon_v$
2. Sufficient coverage of capability frontier
3. KL regularization with appropriate weight

The expected model performance satisfies:

$$\mathbb{E}[\text{Acc}(\mathcal{M}_T)] \geq \text{Acc}(\mathcal{M}_0) + \Omega(T^{1/2}) - O(\epsilon_v \cdot T)$$

This shows linear improvement moderated by verifier quality.

#### 3.7.2 Distribution Preservation

We prove that the KL regularization term ensures:

$$D_{\text{TV}}(p_{\theta_T}, p_{\text{data}}) \leq D_{\text{TV}}(p_{\theta_0}, p_{\text{data}}) + O(\sqrt{\lambda_{\text{reg}}^{-1} \cdot T})$$

where $D_{\text{TV}}$ is total variation distance. This provides formal bounds on distribution drift.

### 3.8 Ablation Studies

Systematically evaluate contribution of each component:

1. **Capability mapping**: Compare adaptive vs. uniform cluster sampling
2. **Quality gating**: Vary ensemble size and threshold values
3. **Curriculum scheduling**: Compare mastery-based vs. linear progression
4. **KL regularization**: Ablate to demonstrate protection against collapse

## 4. Expected Outcomes & Impact

### 4.1 Expected Outcomes

**Primary Outcomes**:

1. **Performance Gains**: We expect 10-20% accuracy improvement over baseline self-improvement methods across all three domains, with strongest gains on complex problems (MATH competition level, difficult programming tasks).

2. **Sample Efficiency**: Achieve target performance with 40-60% fewer synthetic samples compared to uniform generation approaches, demonstrating the value of curriculum-driven selection.

3. **Robustness**: Maintain stable performance over 10+ iterations of self-improvement without model collapse, as evidenced by stable entropy and distribution metrics.

4. **Theoretical Validation**: Empirical results will confirm theoretical predictions about convergence rates and distribution preservation, establishing confidence bounds for practical deployment.

**Secondary Outcomes**:

1. **Transferability**: Demonstrate that models trained with adaptive curriculum transfer better to related domains, indicating more robust learned representations.

2. **Verifier Insights**: Characterize the relationship between verifier ensemble quality and self-improvement success, providing guidelines for verification design.

3. **Open-Source Release**: Publish implementation, trained models, and generated datasets to enable reproducibility and community building.

### 4.2 Scientific Impact

**Advancing Self-Improvement Theory**: This work provides the first comprehensive framework combining curriculum learning principles with self-improvement, bridging these previously separate research areas. The theoretical analysis offers formal guarantees rare in the self-improvement literature.

**Addressing Model Collapse**: By demonstrating principled quality gating and distribution regularization, we provide concrete solutions to the model collapse problem that has plagued self-improvement approaches.

**Verification-Generation Gap**: Our capability mapping explicitly operationalizes the verification-generation gap, providing a practical mechanism for exploiting this gap while respecting verifier limitations.

**Curriculum Learning**: We extend curriculum learning to the unique setting where both problems and solutions are model-generated, requiring novel approaches to difficulty assessment and progression.

### 4.3 Practical Impact

**Scaling Beyond Data Limits**: This framework offers a path to continue improving FMs even as high-quality human data becomes scarce, potentially extending the scaling laws that have driven recent progress.

**Cost Reduction**: By generating targeted synthetic data rather than massive uniform datasets, we reduce computational costs for model improvement by an estimated 40-60%.

**Domain Applications**:
- **Mathematical AI**: Accelerate progress toward IMO-level automated mathematics
- **Code Generation**: Enable models to achieve expert-level programming ability through self-practice
- **Scientific Discovery**: Apply to domains with formal verification (e.g., theorem proving, chemical synthesis planning)

**Education Technology**: Adaptive curriculum principles could inform personalized learning systems for students.

### 4.4 Broader Implications

**Safety and Alignment**: Our quality gating and distribution regularization mechanisms provide concrete tools for maintaining model behavior alignment during self-improvement—a critical safety concern as models become more autonomous.

**Weak-to-Strong Generalization**: The framework demonstrates how weaker verifiers can guide stronger generators, relevant to the alignment problem of supervising superhuman AI systems.

**Understanding AI Learning**: By explicitly modeling capability boundaries and curriculum progression, we gain insights into how foundation models learn and generalize, informing broader questions about AI cognition.

**Responsible AI Development**: We commit to transparent reporting of failure modes, limitations, and potential misuse vectors. The theoretical analysis provides interpretability into when self-improvement is reliable versus when human oversight remains essential.

### 4.5 Limitations and Future Work

**Limitations**:
- Requires tractable verification, limiting immediate applicability to domains with reliable automated evaluation
- Theoretical guarantees assume bounded verifier error, which may not hold for all learned verifiers
- Computational overhead of ensemble verification may limit scaling to very large models

**Future Directions**:
1. **Weak Verification**: Extend to settings with only partial or noisy verification signals
2. **Multi-Modal Self-Improvement**: Apply curriculum principles to vision-language and robotic learning
3. **Meta-Learning Curricula**: Learn curriculum scheduling strategies across problem distributions
4. **Distributed Self-Improvement**: Study multi-agent systems where models collaboratively improve
5. **Safety Specifications**: Incorporate explicit safety constraints into curriculum design
